Abstracts
Conneau et al. (2020) (https://aclanthology.org/2020.acl-main.747)​
The paper shows that pretraining multilingual models at large scale gives big gains on

cross-lingual transfer tasks. Its XLM-R model beats multilingual BERT on XNLI, MLQA, and
NER, with especially strong gains for low-resource languages such as Swahili and Urdu. The
authors also report that multilingual modeling can stay competitive with strong monolingual
models on GLUE and XNLI.
Wang et al. (2022) (https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.03533)​
The paper presents E5, a family of general-purpose text embeddings trained contrastively on a
curated, large-scale set of text pairs (CCPairs), for retrieval, clustering, and classification. It is
evaluated on 56 BEIR and MTEB datasets. Zero-shot, it is the first model to outperform BM25
on BEIR without labeled data, and fine-tuned, it achieves the best MTEB results, beating
models with far more parameters.
Wang et al. (2024) (https://arxiv.org/abs/2402.05672)​
This technical report describes the open-source multilingual E5 models in small, base, and
large sizes. Training follows the English E5 recipe: contrastive pre-training on 1 billion
multilingual text pairs, then fine-tuning on labeled datasets. It also introduces an
instruction-tuned embedding model whose performance is on par with state-of-the-art
English-only models of similar size.
Winata et al. (2024) (https://aclanthology.org/2024.findings-emnlp.155)​
The authors note that how well multilingual language models work for semantic retrieval had
not been thoroughly studied. They introduce MINERS, a benchmark covering bitext mining and
classification via retrieval-augmented context, across more than 200 languages including
low-resource, cross-lingual, and code-switching settings. They report that simply retrieving
semantically similar embeddings is competitive with state-of-the-art approaches, with no
fine-tuning needed.
Niklaus et al. (2024) (https://aclanthology.org/2024.acl-long.805)​
The paper argues that specialized domains like law lack large, high-quality datasets. It releases
MultiLegalPile, a 689GB corpus covering 24 languages and 17 jurisdictions, and pretrains
multilingual and monolingual models on it. The multilingual models set a new state of the art on
LEXTREME, and the English models on LexGLUE. The dataset, models, and code are released
openly.
Lewis et al. (2020) (https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.11401)​
The paper notes that large pretrained models store knowledge but struggle to access and
update it precisely. It proposes retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), which combines a
pretrained generator with a dense retrieval index over Wikipedia. RAG sets state-of-the-art
results on three open-domain QA tasks and produces more specific, diverse, and factual text
than a parametric-only baseline.
Es et al. (2024) (https://aclanthology.org/2024.eacl-demo.16)​
The paper introduces RAGAs, a framework for reference-free evaluation of RAG pipelines. It
addresses the several dimensions that matter: whether retrieval finds relevant and focused

passages, whether the LLM uses them faithfully, and the quality of the generation itself. Its
metrics need no ground-truth human annotations, which speeds up evaluation cycles.

1. Five relevant NLI research papers
1. NLI models for evaluating faithfulness
Steen et al. (2023) — With a Little Push, NLI Models can Robustly and Efficiently Predict
Faithfulness
Abstract summary: The researchers investigated the use of NLI models to determine whether
generated text is supported by its source evidence. They found that NLI-based faithfulness
evaluation can improve through task-specific data augmentation, consideration of both
entailment and contradiction probabilities, and Monte Carlo dropout. Their findings demonstrate
that an NLI model can serve as an efficient faithfulness evaluator when appropriately adapted to
the task.

2. Fine-grained NLI-based faithfulness evaluation
Zhang et al. (2024) — Fine-Grained Natural Language Inference Based Faithfulness Evaluation
for Diverse Summarisation Tasks
Abstract summary: The study examined limitations in existing NLI-based approaches to
evaluating generated summaries. The researchers proposed INFUSE, a method that adjusts the
amount of source evidence considered and simplifies generated sentences into shorter

hypotheses. Their findings showed improved performance across diverse summarization tasks,
highlighting the importance of how source evidence and generated claims are structured during
entailment evaluation.
ACL Anthology
Relevance to CIVIL LEX: Legal explanations can contain several claims supported by different
statutory articles. This study supports evaluating individual claims against relevant evidence
rather than treating an entire answer as one undivided statement.

3. Cross-lingual NLI and hallucination detection
Zhang and Perez-Beltrachini (2024) — Leveraging Entailment Judgements in Cross-Lingual
Summarisation
Abstract summary: This study explored the use of cross-lingual NLI to identify generated
summaries containing information unsupported by their source documents. The researchers
investigated methods for detecting unfaithful content and improving model training to reduce
hallucinations while retaining useful information. Their results support the use of entailment
judgments to assess factual consistency in multilingual generation tasks.

4. Adversarial Natural Language Inference
Nie et al. (2020) — Adversarial NLI: A New Benchmark for Natural Language Understanding
Abstract summary: The authors introduced the Adversarial NLI (ANLI) benchmark, which was
developed through an iterative process involving human annotators and machine-learning
models. Their experiments demonstrated that adversarially constructed examples create a more
difficult test of NLI systems than conventional benchmark examples. The study also showed that
existing models remain vulnerable to challenging inference cases.

ACL Anthology
Relevance to CIVIL LEX: Legal questions often involve exceptions, conditions, and distinctions
between similar statutory provisions. ANLI provides a useful foundation for understanding why
an NLI model should be tested on difficult examples, not only straightforward entailment cases.

5. NLI with faithful explanations
Kumar and Talukdar (2020) — NILE: Natural Language Inference with Faithful Natural
Language Explanations
Abstract summary: The researchers introduced NILE, a method designed to produce NLI
predictions alongside explanations that reflect the reasoning behind those predictions. They
evaluated the method using automated and human assessments and emphasized that
prediction accuracy alone is insufficient to establish the faithfulness of explanations. Their
findings highlight the value of examining whether a model's decisions are genuinely supported
by the evidence used to make them.

ACL Anthology
Relevance to CIVIL LEX: This study supports the importance of evidence-based explanations
and transparent inference decisions in your legal RAG system. It is particularly useful when
discussing why legal answers should be accompanied by relevant statutory citations.
